    building an n-body simulator from nothing: Orbitalis-3D 0.1.0

    how six small steps got from an empty folder to Earth orbiting the Sun, the maths behind each one, and the five things I got wrong on the way

    2026 August 31 · 18 min read · orbitalis-3d / n-body

    the result, first

    scenario   : Sun-Earth, circular, barycentric frame

timestep   : 1.0007 days
steps      : 365  (exactly one orbit)

  Sun    m = 1.988410e+30 kg   x = -4.493143e+05 m   v = -0.089458 m/s
  Earth  m = 5.972168e+24 kg   x = +1.495974e+11 m   v = +29784.647103 m/s

  integrator               final r (AU)   return dist (AU)  rel. |dp| drift
  forward-euler                1.205782       0.886924        3.292e-16
  semi-implicit-euler          1.000007       0.001319        4.308e-17

    Earth goes around the Sun and comes back to within 0.001319 AU of where it started. that is about 197,000 km, on a path 940 million km long: an error of 0.02% of the distance travelled, from a first-order method taking day-long steps.

    and the computed orbital period lands 1.1 seconds away from the real sidereal year. that number cost me an hour and a wrong constant, and it is the best part of this post.

    what Orbitalis-3D is

    a 3D n-body gravitational simulator in C++. it computes the trajectories of planetary systems by numerically solving the equations of motion, and the plan runs from here to a 1.0 that does Barnes-Hut at 100,000 bodies with a live 3D viewer and a binary trajectory format.

    everything is written from scratch. the vector type, the force solver, the integrators, and later the octree. I pull in a test framework and a JSON parser because writing those is not the point, but nothing that touches the physics comes from a library.

    milestone 0.1.0 promised one thing: Earth goes around the Sun once and doesn't fly off into nowhere, and the energy drift is bad and I know it's bad.

    six steps got there.

    0.0.1 build skeleton, three targets, C++20

    the architecture, and the one rule

    three build targets, and the dependency arrow only points one way:

                  orbitalis-core
              static library
        the physics. knows nothing
            about pixels or I/O
                ^         ^
                |         |
      orbitalis-viewer   orbitalis-cli
       real-time 3D       headless runner

    orbitalis-core has zero graphics dependencies and never will.

    that is not tidiness for its own sake. a later milestone benchmarks Barnes-Hut at 100,000 bodies, and those numbers are only trustworthy if they can be produced headless, with no window and no GPU. orbitalis-cli exists to keep that path honest.

    I built the split on day one, before there was anything to separate, because I have done the "split it later when it matters" thing before and later never comes. by the time it matters the graphics code has grown tendrils into the physics and untangling it is a week.

    the maths

    why every number is a double

    this normally gets justified with a vague "floats aren't precise enough". the real number is much more alarming.

    a float has a 24-bit significand. Earth's orbital radius is about $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m, which sits between $2^{37}$ and $2^{38}$. so the gap between consecutive representable floats there is:

    $$2^{37-23} = 2^{14} = 16{,}384 \text{ m} \approx 16 \text{ km}$$

    a float cannot express Earth's position to better than about 16 kilometres. the in-between positions do not exist.

    now the part that kills you. Earth orbits at 29.78 km/s. a sane timestep is well under a second, say $dt = 0.1$ s. per step Earth moves:

    $$29{,}780 \text{ m/s} \times 0.1 \text{ s} = 2{,}978 \text{ m}$$

    2,978 m is smaller than the 16,384 m gap. so position += velocity * dt rounds straight back to the number it started from. the planet does not move. and shrinking dt for accuracy makes it worse, because the step gets smaller while the gap stays fixed. you would be simulating a stationary Earth and hunting for a bug that is not in your code.

    with a double (53-bit significand) the same gap is:

    $$2^{37-52} = 2^{-15} \approx 0.0000305 \text{ m} \approx 31 \text{ micrometres}$$

    thirty micrometres across 150 million kilometres. so double here is not a preference, it is a requirement. floats exist only in the render layer, where a screen is 2,000 pixels across and 16 km of error is orders of magnitude below one of them.

    Newton's law, and why the exponent is three

    the scalar form is the one everyone knows:

    $$F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

    but force is a vector and that only gives its magnitude. to get direction you multiply by the unit vector between the bodies. writing $\vec d = \vec r_j - \vec r_i$ for the separation:

    $$\vec F = G m_1 m_2 \frac{\vec d}{|\vec d|} \cdot \frac{1}{|\vec d|^2} = G m_1 m_2 \frac{\vec d}{|\vec d|^3}$$

    the cube is not a typo and it is where people get confused. it is $1/r^2$ for the physics times $1/r$ for normalising the direction vector.

    and the solver returns accelerations, not forces. every caller immediately wants $\vec a_i = \vec F / m_i$, and that division cancels the $m_i$ Newton's law just multiplied in. so skip both:

    $$\vec a_i = G \, m_j \, \frac{\vec d}{|\vec d|^3}$$

    one fewer multiply and one fewer divide per body, on the hottest loop in the project. it also has a lovely consequence that falls out of the algebra rather than being implemented: the accelerated body's own mass never appears. a grain of dust and a moon at the same distance from the Sun fall at exactly the same rate. that is Galileo's result and the equivalence principle, arriving for free because a symbol cancelled.

    given $d^2 = \vec d \cdot \vec d$, the kernel wants $|d|^3 = d^2 \cdot \sqrt{d^2}$, so: 

    const Vec3   d      = bodies[j].position - bodies[i].position;
const double d2     = d.length_squared() + softening_squared_;
const double inv_r3 = 1.0 / (d2 * std::sqrt(d2));
const Vec3   common = kGravitationalConstant * inv_r3 * d;

accelerations[i] += bodies[j].mass * common;
accelerations[j] -= bodies[i].mass * common;

    one square root and one division per pair. the readable-looking alternative, -G*m/d.length_squared() * d.normalized(), computes length_squared twice, takes the same square root, and adds three divisions, for an identical answer.

    std::pow(d2, 1.5) is also wrong here. pow is a general-purpose function that goes through logarithms; for this specific exponent it is both slower and less accurate than a multiply by a square root.

    each pair is computed once. j starts at i + 1 and the result is applied to both bodies via Newton's third law, so the inner loop runs $n(n-1)/2$ times instead of $n^2$.

    there is a genuine trap in that loop bound:

    for (std::size_t i = 0; i + 1 < n; ++i)   // correct
for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n - 1; ++i)   // hangs on an empty system

    n is std::size_t, which is unsigned. with zero bodies n - 1 is not -1, it is 18,446,744,073,709,551,615. the second version runs approximately forever.

    softening, and being honest about a fudge

    as $|\vec d|$ goes to zero, that acceleration goes to infinity. not "large". infinity, then NaN, and then because every body pulls on every other, one NaN reaches the entire system in a single step. two bodies passing close is not an exotic edge case in an n-body run, it is Tuesday.

    Plummer softening replaces the denominator:

    $$\vec a = G \, m_j \, \frac{\vec d}{(|\vec d|^2 + \varepsilon^2)^{3/2}}$$

    which can never be zero. notice how cheap that is: one addition, no branch, no special case. a large part of why this fix is standard is that it is free in the inner loop.

    what it actually does to the physics is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to treat softening as a bug fix rather than a modelling choice. it is exactly equivalent to replacing each point mass with a Plummer sphere of scale length $\varepsilon$: a smooth blob instead of a point. so at separations well inside $\varepsilon$ the force is deliberately wrong. it does not diverge, it falls to zero. at exactly zero separation the acceleration is exactly zero, which is physically right for the sphere, since at its centre the enclosed mass is zero.

    so: softening does not make close encounters accurate, it makes them not explode. those are different achievements, and the rule that follows is about scale. $\varepsilon$ has to be small compared to the separations that matter.

    • simulating a star cluster, where individual close encounters are noise and you want the smooth collective potential? a generous $\varepsilon$ is correct.
    • simulating solar-system orbital mechanics, where the individual trajectories are the point? $\varepsilon$ should be zero, and close encounters handled by shrinking the timestep instead.

    my measurements: at 1 AU, an $\varepsilon$ of 1,000 km changes the acceleration by 7 parts in $10^{11}$. at a separation of 100,000 km with $\varepsilon = 1{,}000{,}000$ km, it changes the answer completely. same parameter, opposite verdict, and only the ratio changed.

    one thing to carry forward: the potential consistent with this force is

    $$\Phi = -\frac{G \, m}{\sqrt{|\vec d|^2 + \varepsilon^2}}$$

    not the Newtonian $-Gm/|\vec d|$. measuring energy conservation with the unsoftened potential while integrating softened forces would show a drift that is pure bookkeeping.

    the integrators, and one word

    this is my favourite part of the whole milestone.

    given $\vec a = f(\vec x)$, here are two ways to advance a step:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \text{forward Euler:}\quad & \vec x_{n+1} = \vec x_n + \vec v_n \, dt \quad \text{(OLD velocity)} \\ & \vec v_{n+1} = \vec v_n + \vec a_n \, dt \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \text{semi-implicit Euler:}\quad & \vec v_{n+1} = \vec v_n + \vec a_n \, dt \\ & \vec x_{n+1} = \vec x_n + \vec v_{n+1} \, dt \quad \text{(NEW velocity)} \end{aligned} $$

    that is the entire difference: which velocity the position update reads. same two lines, swapped. identical cost, one force evaluation each, the same arithmetic, both first order.

    here is what that one word does to a circular orbit at 1 AU with dt = 1 day:

    radius, started at 1 AU after 1 orbit after 10 after 50 energy swing
    forward Euler 1.2056 1.8944 3.4233 15.3% → 69.0%
    semi-implicit Euler 1.0000 1.0005 1.0022 0.0296%, flat

    why forward Euler always gains. over the step the body genuinely curves inward along its arc. forward Euler moves it along the straight line it was travelling on at the start of the step, which is the tangent, and a tangent always lies outside the circle. so every single step places the body slightly too far out. the error has a sign, and 365 same-signed errors compound into a spiral.

    I have a test asserting the radius is monotonically non-decreasing across a whole orbit, which is a sharper claim than checking where it ended up.

    the closed-form error. with constant acceleration from rest, $n$ steps of $dt$:

    $$ \begin{aligned} \text{exact} \quad & x = \tfrac12 a (n \, dt)^2 = \tfrac12 a n^2 dt^2 \\ \text{forward Euler} \quad & x = a \, dt^2 \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \text{exact} - \tfrac12 a n \, dt^2 \\ \text{semi-implicit} \quad & x = a \, dt^2 \frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \text{exact} + \tfrac12 a n \, dt^2 \end{aligned} $$

    three things fall out, and all three are tested:

    1. the velocity is exact for both. $v = v_0 + at$ is linear in $t$ and both methods integrate it identically. every bit of the error is in position.
    2. the errors are equal and opposite. with $a = 10$ m/s², $dt = 0.1$ s, $n = 100$: exact is 500 m, forward gives 495, semi-implicit gives 505.
    3. so their average is exact. (495 + 505)/2 = 500. not a coincidence, and a hint at where higher-order methods come from: velocity Verlet is roughly what you get by making that averaging structural instead of accidental.

    the error term is proportional to $dt$, which is what "first order" means. halve the timestep, halve the error. RK4 is fourth order, where halving the timestep divides the error by sixteen.

    what "symplectic" actually buys

    it is easy to read "symplectic integrators conserve energy" and take away the wrong thing. they do not conserve the true energy. semi-implicit Euler's energy wobbles by 0.0296% here.

    what it conserves exactly, and forever, is a nearby quantity, usually called a shadow Hamiltonian. the practical consequence is about the shape of the error rather than its size: the error is bounded instead of accumulating.

    my sharpest measurement of this. semi-implicit turns the circular orbit into a slightly eccentric one that breathes between two radii, and I sampled that envelope over different run lengths: 

      1 orbit : lo = 0.991546625472   hi = 1.008749034217
 10 orbits: lo = 0.991546587555   hi = 1.008749321873
 50 orbits: lo = 0.991546586528   hi = 1.008749322028

    between 10 and 50 orbits those bounds agree to ten significant figures, and the residual $1.5 \times 10^{-10}$ is not error growth either: 365 steps of one day is 365.0 days against a period of 365.21, so the sampling phase creeps and longer runs simply land nearer the true extremum. the envelope converges and then stops.

    forward Euler over the same span goes 1.2056 → 1.8944 → 3.4233 and keeps climbing, with its energy swing widening from 15% to 69%. that is not a large error that stays large, it is an error that keeps adding to itself. after a few hundred orbits the planet is gone.

    for this project that distinction is the whole argument for the integrator milestone. simulating the solar system for a million years is a hundred million steps, and any method whose error has a preferred sign destroys the system long before then, no matter how small each individual error is.

    the two conservation laws test different things

    both integrators conserve linear momentum to machine precision. even the bad one.

    that is not luck. each step adds $\vec a_i \, dt$ to every velocity, so the change in total momentum is $dt \sum_i m_i \vec a_i$, and that sum is zero because internal forces cancel in pairs. the ordering of the two update lines never enters the argument.

    so:

    • momentum conservation is a property of the force solver. any integrator doing v += a * dt inherits it.
    • energy conservation is a property of the integrator. the force solver has nothing to do with it.

    which gives a diagnostic rule I expect to lean on for the rest of the project: if momentum drifts, the bug is in the force accumulation. if only energy drifts, it is the method, and it may not be a bug at all.

    I measured the momentum residual in the solver at $\mathbf{1.33 \times 10^{-16}}$ on a four-body system spanning six orders of magnitude in mass. a double epsilon is $2.22 \times 10^{-16}$, so that is about half a ULP.

    it is not zero, and the reason is a nice bit of floating-point pedantry. body $i$ accumulates $m_j \vec c$ and body $j$ accumulates $-m_i \vec c$. weighting by mass gives $m_i (m_j \vec c)$ against $m_j (m_i \vec c)$: the same product, grouped differently. floating-point multiplication is commutative but not associative, so those need not agree bitwise. accumulating forces instead would cancel exactly, at the cost of $n$ extra divisions to recover accelerations. half a ULP is not worth that.

    the two-body reduction, and why the Sun wobbles

    the centre of mass of a set of bodies is the mass-weighted mean position:

    $$\vec R = \frac{\sum_i m_i \vec r_i}{\sum_i m_i}$$

    put the Sun at the origin and Earth one AU away and ask where their common barycentre is. with $M_\odot = 1.9884 \times 10^{30}$ kg, $m_\oplus = 5.9722 \times 10^{24}$ kg, $a = 1.495978707 \times 10^{11}$ m:

    $$R = \frac{m_\oplus \, a}{M_\odot + m_\oplus} = 449{,}314 \text{ m} \approx 449 \text{ km}$$

    the Sun's radius is about 696,000 km. so the barycentre sits 449 km from the Sun's centre, 0.06% of the way to its surface, buried deep inside the star.

    for the Sun and Jupiter the same arithmetic gives 742,000 km, which is 1.07 solar radii. the Sun genuinely orbits a point in open space.

    this matters for how you set up a simulation. the tempting initial condition is: Sun at the origin at rest, Earth at 1 AU with orbital velocity. it is what I would have written without thinking. but the pair then carries a net momentum of $m_\oplus v$, so the barycentre slides at

    $$v_{drift} = \frac{m_\oplus \, v}{M_\odot + m_\oplus} \approx 0.089 \text{ m/s}$$

    nine centimetres a second, which is 2,823 km per year, forever. harmless over one orbit and a growing embarrassment over a long run. worse, it makes "did Earth come back?" ill-posed: back to where, in which frame?

    so the scenario splits position and velocity by the mass ratio:

    const double sun_share   = kEarthGM / gm_total;
const double earth_share = kSunGM   / gm_total;

    each body gets the other body's share. that reads backwards at first glance and is the whole content of the two-body reduction: the heavy body barely moves precisely because the light one is light. the result has total momentum of exactly zero, measured net drift $1.8 \times 10^{-17}$ m/s.

    and it makes something visible that the naive setup hides completely: the Sun moves. it circles a point 449 km from its own centre at 8.9 cm/s, in antiphase with the Earth, and the velocity ratio comes out as $GM_\odot / GM_\oplus = 333{,}000$ to one.

    that wobble is not a curiosity. it is the radial-velocity method, which is how the first exoplanets were found. you cannot see the planet, but you can watch the star being pulled around a point it does not sit at.

    Kepler's third law, and which year

    the period of a circular two-body orbit:

    $$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}}$$

    where $GM$ is the combined parameter of both bodies and $a$ is their separation. using only the central body's $GM$ is the common mistake, negligible for Sun and Earth and catastrophic for a near-equal-mass binary.

    the $a^3$ in there is Kepler's third law: period squared goes as separation cubed. quadruple the separation and the period goes up by eight.

    and then there is the question of which year you are comparing against, because there are three and they are not interchangeable:

    length what it is
    Julian year 365.25 days exactly a unit of time, defined, not measured
    sidereal year 365.256363 days Earth back to the same position against the fixed stars
    tropical year 365.24219 days equinox to equinox, what calendars track

    the sidereal year is the orbital period. the tropical year is 20 minutes shorter because the equinoxes precess: Earth's axis wobbles with a period of about 26,000 years, so the seasons drift backwards relative to the stars. the Gregorian calendar is tuned to the tropical year because we care about when spring is, not which constellation is behind the Sun.

    using the Julian year as an orbital period, which is the obvious mistake given it is the one sitting in most constants files, is 0.0064 days out. that is 9 minutes, and over a thousand-year integration it is 6.4 days of accumulated phase error from a unit confusion rather than any numerical problem at all.

    five things I got wrong

    the maths above is the part that was supposed to be hard. these are the parts that actually cost me time.

    1. vcpkg was quietly linking a scripting language into my binaries

    the first successful build printed, four times:

    'pwsh.exe' is not recognized as an internal or external command

    exit code 0. nothing failed. easy to ignore, and I nearly did.

    there is a vcpkg on this machine with vcpkg integrate install active, which installs a machine-wide MSBuild hook that injects itself into every MSBuild C++ project on the system. from my link line: 

    /LIBPATH:"C:\Users\...\vcpkg\installed\x64-windows\lib"
"C:\Users\...\vcpkg\installed\x64-windows\lib\*.lib"

    that is a wildcard. whatever happens to be installed in vcpkg gets handed to my linker. in my case lua.lib. so my "zero dependencies" core library was linking against a scripting language I never asked for, and the build's inputs depended on the state of an unrelated directory elsewhere on the machine.

    the pwsh error was just vcpkg reaching for PowerShell 7, not installed, before falling back to Windows PowerShell 5.1. cosmetic. the wildcard was the bug. 

    set(CMAKE_VS_GLOBALS "VcpkgEnabled=false")

    project-local, does not disturb the machine-wide integration.

    the lesson: a build that exits 0 is not the same as a build that did what you think it did. I only found this because I went and read a non-fatal message.

    2. my solar mass was 0.026% too high, and it cost 68 minutes of year

    this is the good one.

    the first thing I did in the last step was compute the orbital period properly. I got 365.209506 days against a real sidereal year of 365.256363.

    68 minutes out. over one orbit. that is not rounding, and I spent a while doubting my force law.

    it was the constants. and the way it was wrong is exactly the thing I had written down two steps earlier and then not acted on:

    G is the worst-measured fundamental constant we have, with a relative uncertainty around $2.2 \times 10^{-5}$. except it matters less than it sounds, because nobody actually measures the Sun's mass in kilograms. what astronomers measure is the product $GM$, and that is known to about ten significant figures.

    I wrote that, called it a note for later, and left kSolarMass = 1.98892e30 in the file, copied off a reference table. work out what that mass implies:

    $$ \begin{aligned} G \cdot \text{kSolarMass} &= 1.3274648756 \times 10^{20} \\ \text{measured } GM_\odot &= 1.3271244002 \times 10^{20} \quad \text{(IAU 2009 / JPL DE430)} \\ &\text{high by } 0.0257\% \end{aligned} $$

    and since $T \propto 1/\sqrt{GM}$, being 0.026% high on $GM$ makes the year 0.013% short, which on 365 days is 68 minutes. the arithmetic closes exactly.

    so I turned the constants file inside out. GM is primary, masses are derived:

    inline constexpr double kSunGM   = 1.32712440018e20;
inline constexpr double kEarthGM = 3.986004418e14;

inline constexpr double kSolarMass = kSunGM   / kGravitationalConstant;
inline constexpr double kEarthMass = kEarthGM / kGravitationalConstant;

    which gives a computed period of 365.256350 days against the real 365.256363. 1.1 seconds over a year. a relative error of $3.5 \times 10^{-8}$.

    why GM beats mass is genuinely counterintuitive. you would think mass is fundamental and $GM$ is a derived convenience. for measurement it is the other way round: watch a planet orbit and you directly observe $T^2 = 4\pi^2 a^3 / GM$, pinning down the product to about ten significant figures. to get a mass out you must divide by $G$, which is known to five. converting $GM$ to a mass throws away five digits of accuracy. this is why JPL publishes $GM$ values rather than masses.

    the meta-lesson, which stings more than the physics one: writing an insight into your notes is not the same as acting on it. I identified the precise failure mode two steps early, described why it would matter, and left the wrong number in the file because nothing was measuring it yet.

    3. I kept writing tests that could not fail

    I mutation-test anything numerical: deliberately break the code, confirm the suite notices. three steps running, it found a test that was structurally incapable of failing.

    step the test that looked fine why it was blind
    Vec3 cross(ex, ey) == ez basis vectors zero out the broken term
    System two equal masses balance at the midpoint equal masses make weighted and plain means agree
    gravity acceleration ratio equals mass ratio magnitudes are unchanged by a sign flip

    the pattern is the same every time. I test a derived scalar (a ratio, a mean, a magnitude) when the bug lives in something that scalar throws away (a sign, a weighting, a direction).

    the cross-product one is the clearest. flip a sign in cross and check cross(ex, ey) == ez: it passes, because with basis vectors one term in every component is multiplied by zero, so $a_y b_z - a_z b_y$ and $a_y b_z + a_z b_y$ agree. what caught it were tests on general vectors: anticommutativity, cross-with-self, orthogonality, and the BAC-CAB identity $\vec a \times (\vec b \times \vec c) = \vec b (\vec a \cdot \vec c) - \vec c (\vec a \cdot \vec b)$.

    so the tests that earn their place use ugly asymmetric numbers: 1 kg and 2 kg balancing at x = 2 rather than x = 1.5, and the Sun-Earth barycentre with its 333,000:1 mass ratio.

    4. a failing test that was wrong about itself

    my symplectic-envelope test compared the radius bounds after 1 orbit against 10, and failed by $2.5 \times 10^{-7}$. my first instinct was that the error really does grow slowly and my claim was overstated.

    it does not. 365 steps of one day is 365.0 days against a period of 365.21, so the sampling phase creeps forward each revolution and more orbits simply land nearer the true extremum. the measurement was still converging, not the error growing. comparing 10 orbits against 50 shows the bound stops moving entirely.

    the uncomfortable part: if I had just loosened the tolerance until it went green, the test would still pass and would have quietly stopped meaning anything. loosening a tolerance without understanding why it needed loosening is how a test stops being a test.

    5. I reported a number with no scale

    the first run of the finished CLI printed momentum drift as an absolute value:

      forward-euler          |dp| = 5.278e+13
  semi-implicit-euler    |dp| = 7.662e+12

    which briefly looked like momentum conservation had collapsed, contradicting everything two earlier steps had established.

    it had not. Earth alone carries $m_\oplus \cdot v = 1.8 \times 10^{29}$ kg m/s, so a drift of $5.3 \times 10^{13}$ is a relative error of $3 \times 10^{-16}$. machine precision, exactly as expected.

    I had even written a comment justifying the absolute figure: the barycentric scenario starts at zero total momentum, so dividing by the initial value would divide by nothing. that part was right and the conclusion was wrong. normalising against the largest individual momentum gives $3.3 \times 10^{-16}$ and $4.3 \times 10^{-17}$.

    a number without a scale is not a measurement. I got away with it because I knew what the answer should be. at 100,000 bodies I would not have.

    where 0.1.0 landed

    • Earth returns to within 0.001319 AU of its start after one sidereal year, on a 940 million km path. 0.02% of the distance travelled.
    • forward Euler, identical cost and identical physics, ends up 0.887 AU away and 20% further out. the drift is bad and I know exactly how bad and why.
    • momentum holds to machine precision under both.
    • the computed year is within 1.1 seconds of the real sidereal year.
    • 78 test cases, 269 assertions, zero warnings in Debug and Release.

    six steps: a build skeleton, a vector, a body, a force law, an integrator, an orbit.

    what is next

    milestone 0.2.0 is the live 3D viewer, and I moved it ahead of the serious integrator work on purpose. staring at printed floats for three more milestones would be miserable and I would never catch a bug that way. after that: velocity Verlet and adaptive RKF45, then a Barnes-Hut octree to get from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n \log n)$, then a binary trajectory format.

    the constraint that survives all of it is the one from day one. raylib links into the viewer only. the moment it touches the physics library, the headless benchmarks are dead.
